# Keep the 5:7 split, fix the details

No study tests split-hero proportions. Nobody has measured 50/50 against 40/60 or a golden 38.2/61.8 split on a web page, so the ratio should be chosen for content fit and visual balance, not because one ratio is "proven". The current **5/12 panel (600px of 1440)** is a sound, grid-native choice. It sits between the 1/3 and golden splits and leaves a light, content-bearing field that clearly dominates, which is what both Swiss grid practice and the visual-weight literature favour. The evidence is stronger elsewhere. NN/g eyetracking shows attention leans hard left (**80% of fixations on the left half**) and toward the top of the viewport (**65% of above-fold time in its top half**). First impressions form in **17–50ms** and are driven by low visual complexity and familiarity, not by proportion. Two current settings fail WCAG AA: the 15px descriptions at 60% navy measure about **3.9:1**, and a fixed-height, overflow-clipped hero can break the text-spacing criterion. The largest real problem is on mobile, where nav plus panel take **~49% of the 844px screen before any text appears**. The page needs a set of small corrections, not a new ratio. Shrink the crest slightly. Tighten the eyebrow/lede/link size steps. Bias the text block slightly upward. Get the nav pill off the seam. Cut the mobile panel to roughly a quarter of the screen.

## Every candidate ratio is convention, so pick 5:7 for the grid

The golden ratio has the weakest empirical claim of any candidate. Fechner's 1876 finding that people prefer the golden rectangle came from a 10-rectangle array that later critics showed was methodologically biased. McManus's paired-comparison studies (1980, replicated 2010), the strongest method available, found **population preferences weak but individual preferences strong, stable and highly idiosyncratic** ([Spehar et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00350/full)). Stieger and Swami's three studies, using explicit ratings and the Implicit Association Test, found **no automatic preference for golden-ratio placement** in images ([Westminster Research](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/8yvwz/time-to-let-go-no-automatic-aesthetic-preference-for-the-golden-ratio)). Green's widely cited review is more cautious than dismissive: it finds "real psychological effects" that are "relatively sensitive to careless methodological practices" ([Green 1995](https://www.yorku.ca/christo/papers/goldrev3.htm)). These studies also concern the shape of isolated rectangles, not how a screen divides into two coloured fields. The transfer to a split hero is itself an assumption.

The rule of thirds has no empirical support for layout preference in the notes; it is a photographic convention. The grid canons (Müller-Brockmann, Van de Graaf/Tschichold's 2:3, Bringhurst) carry the authority of craft tradition and internal consistency, not experiments ([Canons of page construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canons_of_page_construction)). Swiss practice does argue for **asymmetric balance with a dominant field** ([Made Good Designs](https://madegooddesigns.com/grid-systems/)). Here the light side carries the entire reading path, so it should be the larger field. That rules out 50/50, which also reads as a "two doors" split-screen, the pattern practitioner guidance associates with dual-path pages ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/best-practices-for-split-screen-design-ad8507d92e66)). This page has one path.

| Split at 1440px | Panel width | Margin around a 400px crest | Grid fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1/3 (4:8) | 480px | 40px each side (cramped) | native |
| Golden 38.2% | 550px | ~75px | off-grid (~4.6 cols) |
| **5/12 (5:7)** | **600px** | **100px** | **native** |
| 1/2 (6:6) | 720px | 160px | native |

Between 1/3 and 1/2 the choice is aesthetic. Because individual preferences are idiosyncratic, the team's own reactions to 36% versus 42% are real but won't generalize to visitors. Keep **5:7**. It is the closest grid-native "near-golden" split (7/5 = 1.4), and it is the only option that gives the crest real breathing room.

## Dark mass on the left lets the panel stay the smaller field

Arnheim's theory holds that visual weight comes from size, value, colour and position. Vision-only judgements do rate darker objects as heavier ([Walker et al., brightness-weight illusion](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/49952/)). The irradiation illusion makes light areas look larger than identical dark ones ([Irradiation illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiation_illusion)). Both effects point the same way. A near-black navy panel reads as heavier and slightly narrower than its pixel width, and the near-white column reads as lighter and wider. Mass-based balance measures predict perceived balance well in simple abstract stimuli: centre-of-mass deviation correlated **r = −0.82 to −0.92** with balance ratings and **r = −0.84** with liking ([Hübner & Fillinger 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00335/full)). The same paper reports McManus's finding that judgements of two grey discs were "incompatible with a physical interpretation of balance." Arnheim cannot be taken literally, and no source gives a compensating ratio.

The practical reading is qualitative. A 50/50 split would put almost all the luminance "mass" left of centre. A 5/12 panel, balanced by a full-strength navy 120px extra-bold wordmark on the right, moves the composition toward equilibrium. The balance runs between two figures, the crest on the left and the wordmark on the right, with the dark field behind them. It is not simply panel against column. Irradiation also makes a light crest on navy look bigger than it is, which argues for a little more panel margin than the arithmetic suggests. Bringing the 400px crest down to about **360px** (60% of the panel width, 120px margins) is an eye-tuned call consistent with this, not a derived number.

The nav link pill that straddles the panel edge works against all of this. It sits exactly on the strongest figure-ground boundary on the page and belongs to neither field. Grid logic says every element should commit to one field. Move the pill fully into the light side, or align it to the text column's left edge.

## Scale, measure and spacing: mostly craft, with a few hard limits

Modular type scales are convention. Bringhurst proposed them and Tim Brown popularized them, justifying his golden-ratio choice on cultural grounds and calling scales tools, not dogma. His own worked example has a headline at **~10.6× body** ([A List Apart](https://alistapart.com/article/more-meaningful-typography/)). Material 3 doesn't use one clean ratio; Display Large to Body Large is **57/16 ≈ 3.6×**, display leading is ~1.12 and body leading 1.5 ([Flutter TextTheme, M3](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/TextTheme-class.html)). The current **120/20 = 6× wordmark-to-lede** ratio sits squarely in poster territory, between those two references, and lands almost exactly on a 1.25 scale from 20px (20×1.25⁸ ≈ 119). Keep it. The weak spot is the small end. Eyebrow 20, link title 18 and description 15 step by only ~1.11 and ~1.2. The eyebrow is the same size as the lede, so weight and colour have to do all the separating. Dropping the eyebrow to about **15–16px** (semibold, small caps or uppercase with positive tracking) restores a legible hierarchy. The wordmark's **0.9 leading and −0.04em tracking** follow display convention (leading and tracking shrink as size grows), though it is far tighter than Material's ~−0.004em. Check "Decision"/"Systems" for collisions.

Measure is a place where evidence and convention openly disagree. Controlled studies find on-screen reading speed flat or *rising* with line length up to ~95–100 cpl, with no comprehension cost. Shaikh and Chaparro found **95 cpl fastest** ([ERAU](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-line-length-on-reading-performance-of-online-news-/)), and Bernard et al. found no reliable speed differences, though people consistently *prefer* moderate lines ([HFI summary](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html)). Bringhurst's **45–75, ideal 66** is a print convention ([webtypography.net](http://webtypography.net/2.1.2)), and WCAG's 80-character cap is an AAA criterion ([W3C 1.4.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/visual-presentation.html)). All of this research uses multi-paragraph prose. A one- or two-line lede barely touches the "losing your place" rationale. Its measure is an aesthetic decision: a balanced break with no widow, and a width related to the wordmark so the column reads as one block. The current **34rem (544px) ≈ 50–55 cpl at 20px** is fine. Add `text-wrap: balance`, and consider capping the lede near the wordmark's own width (roughly 520–560px).

Whitespace has one solid finding. Text with **margins was read faster and comprehended better**, and users were more satisfied, while leading affected only preference ([Chaparro et al. 2004](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/reading-online-text-a-comparison-of-four-white-space-layouts/)). The popular "white space boosts comprehension 20% (Lin 2004)" claim is a miscitation; that paper studied older adults' retention across media ([record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/83/paper-detail/71627)). Baseline grids, 8px systems and scale-derived spacing are consistency conventions with no measured effect. The principle that matters is Gestalt proximity: gaps between groups should be about twice the gaps within them.

Contrast is the one hard constraint. On `#f7f8fa`, navy `#1b2a4a` composites to **6.13:1 at 75%, 4.54:1 at 65% and 3.94:1 at 60%**, against an AA threshold of 4.5:1 for normal text ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); computed in the notes). The lede passes. The 20px semibold eyebrow counts as large text, so it passes. The **15px descriptions at 60% fail** and need at least 70% navy. WCAG 1.4.12 (AA) requires layouts to survive user overrides to 1.5 line-height and 0.12em letter-spacing ([W3C 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)). A one-screen hero must therefore use `min-height`, not a fixed height with `overflow: hidden`.

## Attention leans left and up, and only partly fits this layout

NN/g's 2017 eyetracking, with **120+ participants and 130,000+ fixations at 1920×1080**, put **80% of fixations on the left half**, up from 69% in 2010. When screens grew 900px wider, peak attention moved only about 200px right ([NN/g, Horizontal Attention Leans Left](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-leans-left/)). The same dataset found **57% of viewing time above the fold**, and **65%+ of that time in the viewport's top half** ([NN/g, Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). These are the strongest numbers in this topic. They come from content-rich pages viewed during tasks, though. The left bias partly reflects where information usually sits, and the reversal in right-to-left languages shows it is learned. A text-free navy panel won't absorb 80% of fixations. It will, however, put the crest in the default-attention zone, where it competes with the wordmark for the first look. NN/g's fix for important right-side content is greater prominence, which a 120px wordmark already supplies. Text-left would be the evidence-leaning choice if "read the name first" were the only goal. Since the crest *is* the brand, image-left is defensible. A five-second test would settle which element gets the first fixation.

The F-pattern doesn't apply. NN/g says it appears only on unformatted, text-heavy pages, and calls it a failure mode, not a template ([NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). The Z-pattern/Gutenberg "terminal area" for CTAs has **no primary web eyetracking validation** in the notes ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/dd/2015/04/15/designing-websites-that-mirror-how-our-eyes-work-part-2/)). A sparse hero is closer to NN/g's "spotted" pattern: eyes jump to whatever differs, here the wordmark, the crest and a filled CTA. Decorative imagery tends to be skipped ([NN/g, Photos as Web Content](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)), so nothing essential should live only inside the crest. CTA-position evidence is thin and mostly vendor-grade. One reported 62-person test found buttons placed where reading ends got more fixations ([Econsultancy](https://www.econsultancy.com/do-your-calls-to-action-grab-users-attention/)), which supports CTAs on the column's left edge directly under the lede. Making one CTA visually dominant is reasoned inference, not a tested result. The vertical data mildly argues against strict centring: it is better to bias the headline upward. First-impression research favours the layout as a whole. Appeal forms within **17–50ms** and peaks at **low complexity, high prototypicality** ([Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/); [Lindgaard 2006 via Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html)). Colourfulness "only plays a minor role" ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). A two-field split with one emblem is about as low-complexity as a page can be.

## Mobile is where the current proportions actually fail

At 390×844 the 66px nav plus 350px panel consume **416px (~49%)** before the eyebrow. A rough stack of the text column then puts the CTAs at or past 844px. On iOS Safari with its toolbars showing, the visible area is smaller still, so the lede may be cut off too. Mobile users scroll more readily than desktop users: Baymard found **70% scanned nearly the entire mobile homepage** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/homepage)). But they scroll only with a reason. In one NN/g test of a full-screen hero, **6 of 8 users didn't realize they could scroll** ([NN/g, Illusion of Completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/)). The often-repeated "57% of time on the first *mobile* screen" is a misattribution of desktop data. No study gives an optimal mobile image height, and "image ≤40% of the screen" is convention. Because a crest is a brand identifier rather than decoration, image-first stacking is defensible. It simply has to be short enough for the wordmark to land in the top half. Size the band in **`svh`** so it holds steady as Safari's toolbars move ([web.dev viewport units](https://web.dev/blog/viewport-units)). Hoober's field data shows taps concentrate in the **centre two-thirds** of the screen and calls the popular thumb-zone chart overstated ([Hoober 2014](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2014/09/insights-on-switching-centering-and-gestures-for-touchscreens.php)). Large, centre-spanning CTAs therefore matter more than bottom-edge placement. Targets should be ≥44–48px; WCAG 2.2's 24px is a floor ([W3C 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)). The wordmark is ~64px: "Decision" (8 glyphs, ~4–4.8em) is ~256–307px against a 358px measure, so it fits, but it overflows at 320px widths. Use a rem-plus-vw `clamp()` so 200% zoom still works ([Roselli](https://adrianroselli.com/2019/12/responsive-type-and-zoom.html)).

## Recommended numbers

These are evidence-informed defaults. Where they go beyond the hard limits (contrast, targets, text-spacing tolerance), they are craft judgements to tune by eye.

| Element | 1440×900 | 390×844 |
|---|---|---|
| Nav height | 72px; nav pill fully inside the light field, not straddling x=600 | 56–64px; menu target ≥48px |
| Panel | **600px (5/12)**, full height | **min(240px, 28svh)**, ~25–28% of screen |
| Crest | **~360px** (60% of panel width), optically centred, nudged ~2–3% above true centre | **~150–160px** |
| Text column padding | left **96px** (≈6.7% of width; range 80–112), right ≥96px | **20–24px** gutters |
| Vertical placement | block centred in (900 − nav), then shifted up so top gap : bottom gap ≈ **2:3**; wordmark inside the top half (y ≤ 450) | starts 24–32px below panel |
| Eyebrow | **15–16px** semibold, +0.06–0.1em tracking, navy ≥65% | 13–14px |
| Wordmark | **120px** (clamp ~ `clamp(4rem, 2rem + 6.5vw, 7.5rem)`), lh 0.9, −0.04em, full-strength navy | **60–64px** (≈16vw, test at 320px) |
| Lede | **20px**, lh 1.45, navy 75%, max-width ~**34rem / ≈ wordmark width**, `text-wrap: balance` | **17–18px**, lh 1.45, 3 lines max |
| Link title / description | 18px bold / **16px** at **≥70% navy** (60% fails AA) | 17px / 15–16px at ≥70% |
| Spacing (eyebrow→wordmark→lede→links→CTAs) | **24 / 32 / 40 / 40px**; within link item 4–6px, between items 16–24px | 16 / 20 / 24 / 24px |
| CTAs | **48–52px** tall, one filled primary, one secondary, side by side | **48px**, full width stacked or side by side if both fit at ≥44px |
| Height handling | `min-height: 100svh`, never fixed height + overflow hidden | same; panel in `svh` |

## Conclusion

The research gives this page less leverage over the split ratio than design lore suggests, and more over the things around it. No ratio between 1/3 and 1/2 has measurable advantages. The decisions that do have evidence or standards behind them are the smaller ones: contrast on the grey secondary text, tolerance for text-spacing overrides, top-weighted placement of the headline, and above all mobile panel height, where the current build hands half the first screen to an emblem. Fix those, keep 5:7, and the composition will rest on the parts of the literature that actually hold up.

The open question that matters most is not answerable from the literature: on desktop, does the crest or the wordmark win the first fixation? Left-bias and salience predict the crest. Since that decides whether "brand first" or "name first" is the real experience, a five-second test with a handful of students is worth more than further ratio tuning.
